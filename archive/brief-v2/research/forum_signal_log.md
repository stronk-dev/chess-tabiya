# Forum and community signal log

## Signal A — opening memorization does not yield plans

Repeated questions ask how to “really learn” openings rather than memorize, because players reach the middlegame and no longer know what to do. Other discussions emphasize resulting pawn structures, tactics and plans.

**Product implication:** opening drills must cross the book boundary.

## Signal B — the middlegame is difficult to train

Players describe plenty of opening and endgame resources but few concrete ways to practise quiet or positional middlegames. Several say engine analysis shows an evaluation loss without teaching what the plan was.

**Product implication:** use narrow objectives, multi-move play and branch comparison.

## Signal C — users manually invent fixed-position sparring

Advice commonly recommends taking the end of an opening line, playing several games from both sides, or setting up an ending and playing against a computer.

**Product implication:** the behavior exists; software can remove setup friction and preserve attempts.

## Signal D — practical endgame outcome matters

Users ask for tools that let them play won/drawn positions to completion and specifically value draw-saving exercises.

**Product implication:** win/draw/save must be first-class objectives.

## Signal E — random balanced middlegames have demand

A recent builder used a huge Lichess position set, Maia self-play and Stockfish validation to generate balanced late middlegames, reporting more value than puzzles. Commenters asked for filters by imbalances, center control and king safety.

**Product implication:** corpus+Maia+Stockfish is plausible, but curation and concept filters create the actual product.

## Signal F — UX and latency determine whether drills get used

Community recommendations often point to powerful tools while simultaneously noting confusing navigation, manual setup or poor presentation.

**Product implication:** a technically redundant local implementation can still add real value through fast, coherent workflow.
